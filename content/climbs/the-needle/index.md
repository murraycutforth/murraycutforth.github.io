In my personal mythology, two granite sister crags stand head and shoulders above the rest. Both crags begin with a long slabby apron, which rears up into a vertical bastion split by laser cut corners. Shelterstone crag in the Cairngorms is one, and El Capitan in California (a.k.a. the poor man's shelterstone) is the other. In California I served my apprenticeship for the Needle, climbing El Cap three times. Yosemite big walling is not perfect preparation for Scottish winter climbing, but you could do worse. Instead of dehydration by sweaty walk ins and long days out, you are dehydrated by the baking sun. Rather than scratching around with the pick of an ice axe trying to find positive hooks, you scratch around with strange bits of ironmongery like tomahawks, skyhooks, and cam hooks. Instead of spindrift blinding you at the crucial moment, it's suncream running into your eyes. But the feeling as you commit to a marginal hook or aid placement is the same, as is the length of time a hard pitch takes, the lonely night shift leads, and the all-over tiredness afterwards.

When I moved back to Scotland at the end of 2025, top of my personal wish list was probably the winter Needle. The Loch Avon basin is filled with good memories for me, and while the Needle is a fairly accessible classic in summer, in winter it's a much more mythical beast. I remember seeing a photo decades ago looking up the lower pitches of Citadel, another winter test piece, and thinking how unimaginable it would be to climb that, so the seed was planted long ago. In the days before (and after) trying the Needle me and Rob soaked up a bit more of the history around the route from the descriptions in "The Cairngorms" and "Scotland's Greatest Mixed Climbs" with Andy Nisbet and (dark horse) Colin Maclean employing some advanced tactics on the first ascent- they set off in the afternoon, endured a planned bivi on the terrace, and then topped out at the end of the second day. We employed some advanced tactics of our own. Rob and I were both climbing independently on Carn Etchachan a few days before, so we took the sting out of the approach by stashing a bag of ropes and rack on the top of the Shelterstone. Second, when the forecast gave the go ahead, I spent the evening frantically cutting and sewing the mark 1 prototype of the winter belay kilt, a little bit of kit I hoped would make the long belays slightly more tolerable. We tipped the odds in our favour even more when Michael joined the team.


I still have a 3:40AM alarm on my phone called "THE NEEDLE", and hopefully I'll get some more use from that next winter. We knew there were three hard pitches on the route, and split it into three blocks of 2-3 pitches each. Rob set off at first light, but it was immediately apparent that conditions were not as good as the forecast had promised. Thick wet snow covered everything, while the temperature hovered around freezing, with a concerning amount of water forming on us. Rob had climbed Postern before and identified the start, but almost immediately ripped a tool and landed back on his feet with a tight rope. No problem, take 2. This bulge, which isn't even the crux of a route two grades easier, was putting up a serious fight. Rob gathered more and more momentum on the pitch and flew up the top half. There's a pretty wild move swinging out right from a wide crack higher up this pitch and when seconding I managed to lever my crampon off, resulting in more faff and undignified thrutching. Not a great start overall. The next pitch is absolutely trivial in summer, but involves friction based slab climbing which is impossible in winter. Combined with some long traverses, it felt scary enough on second. These tenuous balance moves with nothing for the axes are not my strong suit. Safely ensconced in a corner, it was time for the first big lead of the day. Another masterclass from Rob, I was still only a few routes in to my winter climbing comeback and the pitch felt continuously desperate on second. The crux traverse involved stepping between crampon smears while pressing the axes into some overhead seams. Good lead. The weather was still continuously poor and combined with the warm temperature, we were all soaked through. The wet snow was melting on contact with us, getting down sleeves and necks with the result that we would have been far warmer on a colder day. Fortunately, there is barely a shred of turf on the route and so the "white and wet" state wasn't a reason to turn around.


The middle block was mine, and off I went. There are a couple of variations, and we chose the layback crack (in summer part of Steeple) rather than the crack for thin fingers. The first introductory section featured some granite weirdness, a fairly gently slab with a corner crack. As is classic on the granite, this slab offered absolutely nothing for the crampons and I ended up torquing my way up the crack while sitting back on my ankles. It was buried at the time, and I was always anxious about finding the next hook. It was really interesting to be back in that exact spot a few months later in summer conditions climbing the spire, and to see that this was a perfect corner crack culminating in a perfect hooky flake. My gloves were soaked, and despite being heavily on the arms I was constantly fighting cold hands, which never seemed to happen before. The meat of the pitch reared up into a steep corner, but the gear was good and the climbing techy but positive so I made slow and steady progress. I just found it oddly difficult to manage the final belly flop off to a ledge on the right. With some relief I brought the others up and set off again up easier ground, questing through deeper snow and a tricky little groove system until I thought I could identify the base of the needle corner in the fading light.


I did feel guilty handing the rack over to Michael as he was faced with the hardest pitch of the route, in the dark, after a long day of wet and miserable belay sessions. But if you're going to put anyone in that position, he's not a bad choice to get the rope up the pitch. Rob and I cracked open the thermos and watched an impressive display of torchlight dancing over the crag while Michael levitated up the wide crack. The temperature plummeted, as Rob said at the time, if only we could magically dry our inner layers, at this point onwards we could have stayed perfectly warm. With every inch gained, the route felt more and more in the bag, and at some point in that indeterminate blob of experience (after darkness falls on a hard mixed route the normal arrow of time doesn't really apply) the call of safe came down. My main memory of climbing that pitch is the total euphoria when I found a Stein pull high up in one of the harder sections. We excavated a tunnel through the eye of the needle and that was that.
